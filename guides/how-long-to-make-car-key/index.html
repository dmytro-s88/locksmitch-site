Quick answer

About an hour at the car for most keys: a spare copied from a working key, a new key when every key is lost, and most push-to-start smart keys. A lockout takes a few minutes. Some European cars take longer, for example a BMW where a module has to be read on a bench: plan for two to three hours. The drive to you comes on top of that, and you get the arrival time by text together with the price. The only real wait is a key that has to be ordered for your exact car.

What happens during that hour?

When every key is lost, the car is opened if it is locked, the lock is read with a decoder, a new key is cut, and the chip or smart key is programmed to the car's immobilizer. Then the lost keys are erased from the car's memory and every button is tested. With a working key the cutting is a copy and the programming is shorter. The full process is in a separate guide: can a locksmith make a key without the original?

How soon can a locksmith get to me?

That depends on where the car is and what time it is. Mitch starts every day in Cumming, so in Cumming and nearby the drive is usually minutes, not an hour. Farther out, for example Atlanta or Marietta late at night, it can be an hour or more. You get the arrival time by text with the price, before anyone drives out, so you can decide.

The car does not have to be at home. Many of our calls come from a work parking lot, a parking deck, a store lot or a car wash, and the key is made right there.

  • Lockouts and all keys lost: 24/7, including nights, Sundays and holidays.
  • Spare keys, programming and other jobs: Monday to Saturday.
  • Nights, 9 pm to 7 am: $50 extra.

Is it cheaper to wait until morning?

Yes, by $50. If the car is parked somewhere safe and you don't need it tonight, a visit after 7 am costs $50 less. If you are stranded, locked out or need the car tonight, we come at night. Either way you get both prices on the phone and choose.

When does it take longer?

  • The key has to be ordered. Some newer cars need a key with the exact part number for that car. Hyundai and Kia smart keys, for example, are matched by a part number that starts with 95440, looked up by the VIN. When we don't carry that key, it is ordered, and the job is done when it arrives. If the car has no key at all, a temporary key can sometimes get it moving first: that is what we did on a 2025 Hyundai Santa Fe in Cumming.
  • A module has to come out. On many BMWs the key data lives in a module that is read on a bench. That adds time, and we say so before we come. More: which BMW key system your car has.
  • The papers are not at hand. We need a photo ID plus the registration, title or insurance card before making a key. If they are locked in the car, we open it first and check them there.
  • The car is not supported. Mercedes-Benz 2015 and newer is a dealer job, and a few brand-new models are not covered by aftermarket tools yet. We check the year, make and model on the phone, so you don't wait for nothing.

How does that compare with the dealer?

A dealer makes keys in its service department. When there is no key, the car has to get there first, which usually means a tow, and then a service appointment and sometimes a key on order. A mobile locksmith brings the blanks, the cutter and the programmer to the car. More on that choice: dealer or locksmith?

How can I make it faster?

  • Send the year, make and model, and whether the car starts with a key or a button.
  • Send the VIN if you can see it: on the driver side of the dashboard, through the windshield, or on the sticker in the driver's door frame.
  • Say where the car is parked, and send a photo of any key you still have, even a broken one.
  • Have your photo ID and the registration, title or insurance card ready.
