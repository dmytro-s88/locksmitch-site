Can I program a key fob myself?Sometimes. Which cars allow it, and where a programmer is the only way.

Some cars have an owner procedure printed in the manual: with the right working keys in hand, you add another one in a few minutes. Many others have none, and no sequence of key turns will change that. Here is how to tell which car you have.

Quick answer

Sometimes. Many Ford models, many Chrysler, Dodge and Jeep models with Sentry Key, and many Chevrolet models let the owner add a key with no tool, usually only when one or two working keys are already in hand. The exact steps are in the owner's manual for your year. Toyota, Honda and Nissan keys, most push-to-start smart keys and nearly every case with no working key need a programmer. Two jobs hide in one fob

A key can do two separate things, and they are programmed separately on many cars:

  • The remote: the lock, unlock and trunk buttons.
  • The immobilizer chip: the part the car checks before it lets the engine start.

A procedure that pairs the buttons does not make the car start. When you read instructions online, check which of the two they cover.

Cars where the owner can add a key

These are the cases we could confirm in the maker's own owner's manuals. Model years differ, so the manual for your exact car decides.

Ford: with two working keys

Ford's support site says you can program a spare yourself if you already have two previously programmed keys, and points to the spare key programming section of the owner's manual. It covers keys with a blade and, on models where the manual has it, push-to-start Intelligent Access keys. A key with a blade must be cut first. With one working key or none, Ford sends you to the dealer; a locksmith with a programmer does the same job at your car.

Chrysler, Dodge, Jeep: with two valid Sentry Keys

Owner's manuals for many models from the 2000s and early 2010s have a section called "Customer Key Programming". With two valid Sentry Keys you can program a new blank key after it is cut. The manuals add that a key programmed to one vehicle can't be programmed to another, so a used key from a different car won't work.

Chevrolet: with one or two recognized keys

Chevrolet owner's manuals have a "Programming Keys" section. On some years one recognized key is enough to add another. On later years it takes two. The same manuals describe a slower procedure for US vehicles when no recognized key is available: about 30 minutes, three waits of 10 minutes each, with a correctly cut key. The manuals warn that after it, all previously known keys stop working until they are learned again. Manuals for push-to-start models describe similar steps for the transmitters. Other GM brands: check your own manual for the same section.

Older remotes with buttons only

On many older cars with a separate remote, the manual gives a timed sequence of ignition turns that puts the car in programming mode, after which you press a button on each remote. Ford manuals describe exactly this and warn that every remote must be programmed in the same session: the ones that are not present stop working.

Where it is not possible without a programmer

  • Toyota. Toyota's warranty and maintenance guides say only a Toyota dealer or certain registered locksmiths can generate replacement keys, and Toyota support says a key with a chip is programmed to the car with a scan tool.
  • Honda. Honda owner's manuals give no owner procedure: for a replacement key or remote they send you to the dealer to have it programmed.
  • Nissan. Nissan says new keys must be registered to the car, and that registration erases the keys already stored, so every key has to be present. Nissan recommends its dealer for this.
  • Push-to-start smart keys, unless your manual has a procedure. Some Ford and Chevrolet manuals do.
  • No working key at all. The Ford and Chrysler procedures need two working keys. The Chevrolet 30-minute procedure is the exception, where the manual has it, and it still needs a key cut for the car.
  • Any car whose manual has no programming section. No section means no owner procedure.

"Dealer" in a manual does not mean dealer only. A mobile locksmith with the right programmer does the same registration at your car. The real exceptions for us are listed further down.

Plug-in programmers from the store

AutoZone and Home Depot sell do-it-yourself key kits with a small programmer that plugs into the car's diagnostic port. The product pages are clear about the limits: they work on select vehicles only, you need at least one working key, the tool locks to one car after use, and a key with a blade still has to be cut. Check the compatibility list for your exact year before you buy.

Before you try it yourself

  • Read the manual section for your exact year first. Steps and timings differ between years of the same model.
  • Count your working keys. A key that only opens the door doesn't count. It has to start the engine.
  • Match the part. The new key or fob has to be the right one for your car and new, not used: bought a key online?
  • Have the blade cut first. The new key has to turn in the ignition for the procedure to work.
  • Bring every key and remote you own. Some procedures erase the ones that are not there.
  • If it fails twice, stop. Repeating a procedure that erases keys can leave you with fewer working keys than you started with.

Common questions

Can I program a key fob myself?

On some cars, yes. Many Ford models, many Chrysler, Dodge and Jeep models with Sentry Key and many Chevrolet models have an owner procedure in the manual, usually requiring one or two working keys. If your owner's manual has no programming section, the car needs a programmer.

Can I program a Ford key myself?

Yes, if you already have two working programmed keys. Ford's support site and owner's manuals describe the procedure in the spare key programming section. With one working key or none, it takes a programmer.

Can I program a Chevy key myself?

Often, yes. Chevrolet owner's manuals have a "Programming Keys" section: some years need one recognized key, later years two. With none, the manuals for US vehicles describe a 30-minute procedure that erases all previously known keys. Check the manual for your exact year.

Can I program a Dodge, Jeep or Chrysler key myself?

On many models from the 2000s and early 2010s, yes, with two valid Sentry Keys. The owner's manual section is called "Customer Key Programming". The new key must be cut first and must never have been programmed to another vehicle.

Can I program a Toyota, Honda or Nissan key myself?

No. Their owner's manuals give no owner procedure for a key with a chip. The key is registered to the car with a programmer, by a locksmith or the dealer.

Can I program a key myself with only one working key?

On Ford and on Chrysler, Dodge and Jeep, no: the owner procedures need two. On some Chevrolet years one recognized key is enough. Otherwise it takes a programmer, from $99 with LocksMitch for a key you already have.

Do the plug-in key programmers sold in stores work?

On the vehicles they list, when you have at least one working key. They are limited to select vehicles and the tool locks to one car after use. Check the compatibility list for your exact year, make and model before buying.

How much does it cost to have a key fob programmed?

With LocksMitch in Cumming and North Atlanta: from $99 for a key or fob you already have, cutting included, $129 for a push-to-start smart key and from $89 for a remote with buttons only. It is done at your car, and the price is quoted before anyone drives out.

What if I tried it myself and it didn't work?
